Nearly everyone in Reed’s Petrograd called themselves socialist, a council or a committee, and they stood on both sides of the barricades. Each entry gives Reed’s own words for it, where it stood on the rising of 7 November, and its leading people. About this map

John Reed’s Ten Days That Shook the World (1919), scene by scene, over today’s street plan of Petrograd. It follows Reed: his path, what he saw, what he was told and what he printed, as he wrote it. It is his account, sympathies and all, not a history of the revolution, and nothing in it is corrected from other sources. Where he leaves a gap another source may fill it, and the card then says “Not in Reed”.

What happened

In March 1917 a revolution ended the Tsar’s rule, and power was split between a Provisional Government and the Soviets, the councils of workers and soldiers. On 7 November (25 October in the old Russian calendar) the Bolsheviki took power through the Petrograd Soviet. Reed’s book follows those days and the weeks after; the introduction tells it at more length.

The people

  • Every body of people on the map is one that Reed puts there: the scene’s quotation, or another passage of his, names it. It stays until his text removes it: it marches off, it is disarmed, or the building it holds falls to the other side. A passing crowd stands only for its scene. The card lists who is on the scene, in Reed’s words.
  • Each body stands on a patch of its side’s colour. Defenders of a building face out from its walls, and everybody else faces it, or whatever Reed has them look at (“staring at the hotel roof”). A body Reed puts indoors (“they are in the back rooms”) is a label on its building.
  • Where Reed says how a body stands, it stands so in that scene (a cordon stays a line until his text removes it): “an interminable column of soldiers” up the Nevsky, “a long double file of cyclists”, “a line of soldiers, rifles ready”, “three companies of long-coated yunkers” drawn up in ranks. A column he sees marching past walks down the street while the scene lasts, in step or, if he says “irregular” or “out of step”, not. Which way it walks is ours unless he gives it (“up the Nevsky”, read as he uses it, away from the Admiralty).
  • The figures are not a head count: Reed’s “two thousand Red Guards” are drawn as about 140, a crowd as 60. Shapes follow photographs of Petrograd in 1917: the soldier’s long belted greatcoat and fur papakha, the Women’s Battalion’s light fur hats, the sailors’ peakless caps, the peasants’ foot-cloths. Colours follow Reed where he gives them (“the red shoulder-straps of the yunker-schools”, the “elephant-coloured” armoured cars); the photographs are black and white, so the rest are conventional. One body is ours too: who “bloodily recaptured” the Military Hotel and the Telegraph Agency Reed does not say, and the map draws sailors, who had held the hotel. Two touches are ours, not the record’s: the red arm-band that tells a Red Guard from the crowd, and the machine-gun belts some sailors wear crossed on the chest.
  • The fighting is drawn only where Reed has a body fire, and as he has it. Riflemen kneel or stand to fire at the part of the enemy nearest them; machine guns and armoured cars fire bursts; field-guns recoil and burst their shells on the walls, and the holes stay there in every later scene. A garrison under siege fires from its windows. Troops that “surrounded the building” stand all round it; an assault comes in waves that “crumpled” and fall back, or that “poured through” the doors; armoured cars cruise “back and forth”; the dead and the wounded lie where he puts them, and people who throw themselves flat lie on their faces. A shot he only hears (“a burst of rifle-fire not far off”) is not drawn, and neither is a shooter he does not see.
  • The camera opens each scene on a frame that takes in everything it names, then comes in close, where the people are drawn at twice life size, to each place where the scene’s own are, and ends close on the first; a walk or a ride it follows first, close behind, and comes down where it ends. A touch on the map hands it back to you.
  • A fight plays out. Troops who come into it to fire or to storm come up from behind their places in rushes, running, dropping to fire and running on, their guns hauled up by the crews and their armoured cars driving up with them; guns and cars Reed has arrive (“three field-pieces arrived”, “a Bolshevik armoured car appeared”) come up, and the storming waits for the car; men who take a building march up to it, and the other side’s people there run off; the relief at the Telephone Exchange walks into the “blast of rifle-fire” and falls; the patrol with the ultimatum sends three to the Vladimir school’s door and back; the yunkers “driven back” come out of their door toward the fire and run back in; the sailors who “rushed” the stalled car close round it. Cars, trucks, cabs, trams and armoured cars stop for the people in their way: those standing about step aside and back, marchers are waited for, and a crowd that throws itself flat as a car comes lies in the gutters, as Reed has it. They steer round guns, barricades and other vehicles; where there is no way round, a machine gun is wheeled aside and a barricade pulled open for them, and put back after. Who is there, whom they fire at and what becomes of them are Reed’s; the way they come and the timing are the map’s.
  • The Congress and its walk-outs are drawn inside Smolny’s great hall. In a scene there Smolny is cut away over the hall, as in an architect’s section, and the hall is laid out inside: benches in three blocks with aisles, the platform with the presidium table and the reading stand, the door at the back, and the “white chandeliers” Reed names. He does not say where the hall is in the building or how big it is: its place, its size and the platform at its south end are the map’s, and it is drawn at street level. So is who sits where: the factions in blocks, those for the rising on the chair’s left and those against on its right. Their numbers follow the presidium Reed says was chosen “upon proportionality” (“14 Bolsheviki, 7 Socialist Revolutionaries, 3 Mensheviki and 1 Internationalist”), four figures to a seat; the split of the seven between the Left Socialist Revolutionaries and the rest, and the Mensheviki Internationalists and the Bund, are the map’s. By those proportions more walk out than Reed’s “fifty delegates got up and pushed their way out”: his two counts do not agree, and the map keeps the factions whole. A faction that walks out rises, goes down an aisle and out at the back, while the hall hoots or cheers as Reed has it; one that comes in walks up onto the platform. The card names the speaker at the reading stand and who walks out or in. The other Smolny sessions are drawn there too: the Petrograd Soviet, with “the ‘moderate’ Socialist intellectuals, officers, members of Army Committees, the Tsay-ee-kah” “there in force” (drawn as one body under the old Tsay-ee-kah’s name); the hall filling with “soldiers and workmen, a monstrous dun mass” for the old Tsay-ee-kah’s last session; Trotzky leaving the platform (“I’m going now!”) down the side of the room; the press decree’s vote, drawn one figure to a vote (“34 to 24”), the minority as Left Socialist Revolutionaries though Reed puts two Bolsheviki among them, and all the Bolsheviki hooting though “a part” applauded; and the Peasants’ Congress coming in, its presidium and the Tsay-ee-kah’s embracing on the platform, the two banners hung crossed on the white wall over the empty frame “from which the Tsar’s picture had been torn”, once both presidiums are up. A speaker who comes in with those walking in comes in last of them: the map’s. Who sits where in these is the map’s too, and so is the frame’s being there from the first of them: Reed mentions it only at the last.
  • The City Duma’s two halls are drawn the same way. Reed’s are “the galleried Alexander Hall, hung with its great, gold-framed, red-shrouded Imperial portraits” and “the high, amphitheatrical Nicolai Hall”; the Committee for Salvation formed as “about a hundred people were grouped around the platform, where Skobeliev was speaking”; the Bolsheviki leaving the chamber, “followed by cries of ‘German agents! Down with the traitors!’”; the “imprecations from the right” at Schreider; and at the Peasants’ Congress the soldiers on the left, “a sprinkling of officers’ epaulettes” on the right, “a few peasants, non-commissioned officers, and some soldiers” in the centre, “the galleries … thronged with workers”, the old Executive Committee leaving the hall and Zinoviev hooted down. The map’s: where the halls are in the building, their sizes and the end their platforms are at; two blocks of benches, the halls being narrow, the centre’s seated first by the centre aisle and each side then filled file by file from its wall, so that the hall reads from left to right (the Left Socialist Revolutionaries, “more than half”, fill the left and spill over the aisle); one gallery, along the far wall, and its height and depth; two portraits, either side of the platform; the height of the Nicolai Hall’s steps; the Duma’s numbers and the Bolsheviki’s (30 and 10, the Duma as many as its side’s benches hold), and their sides; the Committee and the Peasants’ Congress drawn about one figure to six people (Reed’s “about four hundred” and “more than half”, “a bare fifth”, “a quarter”); that all but the Bolsheviki hoot Zinoviev (Reed’s “most of the delegates were hostile”), and that the workers in the gallery do not; the dress of the others on the right and in the centre beyond Reed’s officers, peasants and soldiers. The Bolshevik benches laugh (“ironic laughter”), leaning back and shaking; Zinoviev’s hooting is drawn, the laughter as he leaves is not. Tchernov’s return is drawn too: the Bolsheviki “on their feet all at once, shaking their fists and yelling”, and Tchernov leaving (“This is an outrage, not a session!”). Reed dates his arrival “the afternoon of November 25th” and says only “Tchernov entered the hall”: the session’s day and that it was in the Alexander Hall are the map’s, and so is the seating, carried over from the first session. Not drawn: “some of the Left Socialist Revolutionaries” on their feet with the Bolsheviki (only part of a body), “the rest of the assembly tried to yell them down”, Zinoviev’s heckling, and the old Executive Committee he brought back.
  • The Imperial Law School at Fontanka 6 has its “great meeting-hall” drawn the same way, for the Congress’s last two sessions there. Reed’s: Tchernov at the tribune, “interrupted by a great burst of laughter”, who “left the platform and the room”; the extraordinary session, and the “arrival of a delegation from Smolny, accompanied by representatives of the Red Army, greeted with a rising ovation”. The map’s: where the hall is in the building (the west wing, along the Fontanka), its size and the end its platform is at; the delegates’ number, about one figure to six of Reed’s “about four hundred”, filling the hall from the front; the delegation’s eight, seated in the middle of the front rows; that nobody stands at the tribune in the second session (Ustinov, who announced the delegation, is not in the Faction Map). The old Executive Committee’s rump convention “in another room of the same building” is not drawn. A hall laughing at its speaker laughs as a hall, all but the speaker’s own party: leaning back from the waist, the hands at the belly, shaking for six seconds, then as before.
  • Rifles and guns burnt smokeless powder in 1917, so what hangs over a fight is dust and plaster and the smoke of bursting shells, not clouds of gunsmoke. Fires burn after dark.

Time

  • Dates are New Style, as Reed writes them: “I have adopted in this book our Calendar throughout.” Where a document he prints uses the Old Style, the card says so.
  • Reed gives about sixty clock times in the whole book. Where he gives one, the card shows it plainly; “c.” marks a time read from a phrase like “toward evening” or estimated from the order of events, and the card says which.
  • The sky is computed for each scene’s date and hour on the Petrograd clock of 1917: Pulkovo mean time plus the summer hour, about UTC+3:01. November light is short; most of the revolution happens in the dark.
  • The city turns white after the morning of 13 November, when Reed sees the first snow.

The map

  • The base map is today’s OpenStreetMap, capped at six storeys. Bridges built after 1917 (Betancourt, Alexander Nevsky) are removed.
  • The tour stays in Petrograd. Reed’s scenes at the front (Pulkovo, Tsarskoye Selo, Gatchina) and in Moscow are in the data and its checks, but not on this map; a move that leaves the city is drawn as an arc toward where it went, and the camera keeps to the city.
  • The detailed city runs from the Petrograd Side to Smolny. The southern gates and the factories beyond it are pins on the plain.
  • Reed never says where he lodged, so his walks from home start nowhere on the map.

Petrograd · 1917

The revolution of November 1917

By 1917 Russia had been at war with Germany for three years. In March a revolution in Petrograd, the capital, ended the rule of the Tsar. Power passed to two bodies at once: a Provisional Government of ministers, led from July by Alexander Kerensky, and the Soviets, councils elected by workers and soldiers.

The war went on, and bread and land did not come. By the autumn the Bolsheviki, led by Lenin and Trotsky, had a majority in the Petrograd Soviet. On the night of 6 to 7 November its Military Revolutionary Committee took the Telegraph Agency, the Post Office and the Telephone Exchange, and put a cordon round the Winter Palace, which fell in the early hours of the 8th. A Congress of Soviets took power, and Kerensky’s attempt to win the city back with Cossacks failed within a week.

Russia still kept the old calendar, thirteen days behind ours, so these November days are the “October Revolution”. The map uses our dates, as Reed does.

Who is who

Two headquarters, and everyone between

  • For the rising: the Bolsheviki and their allies the Left Socialist Revolutionaries, the Soviet’s Military Revolutionary Committee, the factory Red Guards, most of the garrison and the sailors of the Baltic Fleet. Their headquarters was Smolny.
  • The Provisional Government and those who fought for it: Kerensky and his ministers in the Winter Palace, the officer cadets Reed calls “yunkers”, General Krasnov’s Cossacks and the Women’s Battalion.
  • Against the rising, without being the government: the City Duma, the moderate socialists (Mensheviki and Socialist Revolutionaries), the liberal Cadets, and their Committee for Salvation.
  • Neutral or split: regiments that would not choose, the Cossacks in the city, and the railwaymen’s union.

On the map a building takes the colour of whoever holds it at that moment. Reed himself is the black figure, pale at night.

History as he saw it

This book is a slice of intensified history—history as I saw it.
Reed, Preface

John Reed (1887–1920) was an American journalist and socialist. He reached Petrograd with his wife, Louise Bryant, in September 1917, and wrote down what he saw, what he was told, and what he read in the papers and on the walls. “In the struggle my sympathies were not neutral,” he says. The map keeps his side of the story: his path through the city, scene by scene, as he wrote it.
