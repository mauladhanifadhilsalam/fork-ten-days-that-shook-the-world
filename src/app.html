About this map

John Reed’s Ten Days That Shook the World (1919), scene by scene, over today’s street plan of Petrograd. It follows Reed: his path, what he saw, what he was told and what he printed, as he wrote it. It is his account, sympathies and all, not a history of the revolution, and nothing in it is corrected from other sources.

Buildings that change hands are tinted by who holds them at that moment, as Reed reports it: for the insurrection, the Provisional Government, the opposition (Duma, Committee for Salvation), neutral.

Time

  • Dates are New Style, as Reed writes them: “I have adopted in this book our Calendar throughout.” Where a document he prints uses the Old Style, the card says so.
  • Reed gives about sixty clock times in the whole book. Where he gives one, the card shows it plainly; “c.” marks a time read from a phrase like “toward evening” or estimated from the order of events, and the card says which.
  • The sky is computed for each scene’s date and hour on the Petrograd clock of 1917: Pulkovo mean time plus the summer hour, about UTC+3:01. November light is short; most of the revolution happens in the dark.
  • The city turns white after the morning of 13 November, when Reed sees the first snow.

The map

  • The base map is today’s OpenStreetMap, capped at six storeys. Bridges built after 1917 (Betancourt, Alexander Nevsky) are removed.
  • The detailed city runs from the Petrograd Side to Smolny. Places beyond it, like the southern gates, the front at Pulkovo, Tsarskoye Selo and Gatchina, are pins on the plain; Moscow and the far stations get a card.
  • Reed never says where he lodged, so his walks from home start nowhere on the map.
